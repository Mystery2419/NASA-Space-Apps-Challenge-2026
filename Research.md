R Draconis:
About:
It is an old red giant star that is located in the northern circumpolar constellation Draco, it is also known as a Mira-type pulsating star. The outer layers of the star begin to swell and contract drastically every eight months, this changes the brightness of the star for a long period of time. The star ran out of its Hydrogen fuel a long time ago, causing it to expand and cool into a red giant. The surface temperature of the star is between 2400K-3700K, in comparison to the Sun being 5,778K. The cool temperature gives the star a deep orange-red glow.
Strong stellar winds and pulsations will strip the outer gas layers of the star, it into space, over the next few indeed thousand years, this will form a glowing planetary nebula. The star will be left as a dense white dwarf core.
The Star has a pulsation period, taking 245.2 days to complete one full contraction and expansion per cycle.
The brightness ranges from 7.5 at peak brightness to 11.3 at the dimmest. R Draconis is too faint to be seen by the naked eye, at peak brightness you must use binoculars and at the dimmest a backyard telescope must be used.
The estimated mass is between 1.0-1.5 solar masses. R Draconis started out as a star larger than our Sun, however strong stellar winds have shed the outer layers of then star into space.
The luminosity is approximately between 3,000 and 5,000 solar luminosities. It emits most of its energy as visible infrared heat rather than visible light, the total energy output is thousands times brighter than the Sun.
By using Weins law and R Draconis’ cool temperatures, we can predict the peak energy output into the near-infrared. R Draconis is observed in infrared bands, for example: 2MASS, IRAS and WISE. They allow astronomers to capture the true bolometric luminosity.
How the pulsation works briefly: There is an internal gas layers beneath. The surface that traps heat, and builds up pressure and radiation underneath the layer, which eventually means the pressure overcomes the gravitational forces, pushing the atmosphere of the star outwards, the radius of the star will increase. The gas will expand and cool, electrons will combine with atoms. When electrons combine, The trapped heat is released into outer space causing the internal pressure to drop again, so the layers go back down, restarting the pulsating cycle.

Spectral types:
A spectral type is also known as a spectral classification, it is an astronomical system used to categorise stars based on surface temperature, chemical elements detected in their light and colour.
The temperature letters: O,B,A,F,G,K,M
O and B are extremely hot, massive blue-white stars with a range of 10,000 to over 30,000 degrees.
A are hot white stars with a range 7,500-10,000 degrees.
F and G are yellow-white to yellow stars. The sun is a G type star, the temperature is around 5,500 degrees.
K are cooler orange stars with a temperature ranging from 3,500 to 5,000 degrees M are the coolest of stars, they glow red or a deep orange with temperatures of around under 3,500 degrees.

Number subdivisions (0-9)
0 is defined as the hottest and 9 as the coolest, so M1 will be warmer than M8 at the M category.

Luminosity cases
Done in roman numerals, shows the size and brightness of the star.
I: supergiant
II:Bright giant
III:Regular giant
IV:Sub-giant
V: Main sequence stars

Special letters (for example “e”)
Lowercase letters at the end show unusual features in the star’s light spectrum. So the “e” is for emission lines, so the star has a glowing gas around it. The bright emission lines in this case are formed from the superheated surrounding hydrogen gas. This is formed by the outward pulse sending shockwaves that crash into the thin upper atmosphere of the star, superheating hydrogen.
